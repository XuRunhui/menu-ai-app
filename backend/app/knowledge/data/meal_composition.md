# How dishes are combined into a meal

Menuist editorial notes used as retrieval context for combo recommendations. General culinary
principles and meal customs, written for this project.

## Balancing tastes

Cooks balance the five basic tastes (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami) together with heat from
chili and the weight of fat. Rich, fatty, fried, or cheesy dishes feel lighter next to something
acidic (pickles, vinegar dressings, citrus, fermented vegetables), something fresh (raw herbs,
crisp salads), or something bitter (leafy greens, beer, coffee, tea). Spicy heat is soothed by
cooling or starchy companions: yogurt and other dairy, rice, bread, cucumber, or a sweet drink.
Sweet pairs well with salty or sour, as in sweet-and-sour sauces or salted caramel, but two very
sweet items together feel cloying. Several umami-heavy dishes (broths, soy sauce, aged cheese,
tomato, mushrooms, cured meat) amplify each other, so one umami-rich dish is usually offset by a
lighter or more acidic one. A shared ingredient or herb can also bridge two dishes, for example
cilantro in both a salsa and a curry.

## Texture and mouthfeel

Contrast keeps a meal interesting: crisp or crunchy food next to something soft, creamy, or saucy
(fries with a dip, fried chicken with coleslaw, tempura with rice and miso soup). A saucy, brothy,
or gravy-covered dish wants something to soak up the sauce, such as rice, bread, or fries. A meal
where every item is fried, or every item is soft and mushy, feels monotonous.

## Temperature

Hot main dishes are often paired with cold or room-temperature sides and drinks: pickles, salads,
kimchi, raita, coleslaw, a cold beer or soda. The contrast refreshes the palate between bites.

## Color and appearance

People eat with their eyes first. Fried, braised, and grilled foods tend to be brown or golden, so
they look more appetizing next to green (herbs, vegetables), red or orange (tomato, peppers, chili),
white (rice, yogurt, dairy), or purple (pickled onion, red cabbage). Some cuisines make color a
rule: Japanese home cooking aims for five colors in a meal (white, black, red, yellow, green), and
Korean cooking uses the obangsaek palette of five colors (white, black, red, yellow, blue-green),
visible in bibimbap and in an assortment of banchan.

## Weight, portion, and cooking methods

A good combo pairs one heavy dish with lighter ones; two heavy mains are better shared by a group.
For one person a combo is usually a main plus a side or starter, often with a drink. Varying the
cooking method across dishes (raw, steamed, simmered, grilled, fried) also adds contrast;
Japanese cooking lists these methods as a guide for planning a varied meal.

## Chinese meals

Everyday Chinese meals are served family style: several shared dishes eaten with plain rice (or
noodles or steamed buns in the north). A balanced table mixes meat or fish with vegetables, often
adds a soup, and varies cooking methods and flavors, so a spicy or fried dish sits next to a mild,
steamed, or stir-fried vegetable. Traditional food beliefs also balance "heating" and "cooling"
foods. Dim sum brunch is a spread of small shared plates (steamed dumplings, buns, rice rolls,
fried items) accompanied by tea, which cuts through oiliness.

## Japanese meals

The classic home meal, ichiju-sansai ("one soup, three dishes"), is rice, a soup such as miso soup,
one main protein dish, two smaller vegetable dishes, and pickles (tsukemono). Bento boxes follow a
similar balance of rice, protein, vegetables, pickles, and color in a compact box.

## Korean meals

A Korean meal centers on rice (bap) and a soup or stew (guk or jjigae), surrounded by shared banchan
side dishes such as kimchi, seasoned vegetables, and pickles. Fermented, sour banchan cut the
richness of grilled meat, and spicy dishes are balanced by mild ones.

## Indian meals (thali)

A thali serves rice and/or flatbread with small bowls of dal, vegetable dishes, a meat or paneer
curry, yogurt or raita, pickle or chutney, and a sweet. Traditional thinking aims to include all six
tastes of Ayurveda (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, pungent, astringent) in one meal. Cooling raita or a
yogurt drink such as lassi balances spicy curries; crisp papad adds crunch.

## Southeast Asian meals

Thai meals are shared with rice and balance sour, sweet, salty, and spicy flavors across several
dishes, for example a curry, a stir-fry, a tangy salad, and a soup. Malaysian and Indonesian nasi
campur puts small portions of meat, vegetables, egg, and sambal around a mound of rice. Vietnamese
meals pair grilled or fried items with fresh herbs, lettuce, and dipping sauces such as nuoc cham.

## Middle Eastern and Mediterranean meals

Meze is a spread of small shared dishes: dips such as hummus and baba ghanoush, salads, pickles,
cheese, olives, and grilled meat, eaten with flatbread and often with tea or an anise spirit.
Spanish tapas are small plates shared over drinks, mixing fried, cured, and fresh items.

## Italian meals

A traditional Italian meal moves through courses: antipasto (starter), primo (pasta, risotto, or
soup), secondo (meat or fish) served with a separate contorno (vegetable side), then dolce. A rich
primo is usually followed by a simpler secondo, and vegetables balance the meat course.

## French meals

A French meal typically has an entree (starter), a plat principal (main course), then cheese and/or
dessert. A rich main is often preceded by a light starter such as a salad, and bread accompanies the
meal.

## Mexican meals

Tacos, grilled meats, and stews come with salsas, lime, chopped onion, cilantro, and often rice and
beans. Fresh salsa and lime add acidity to rich meat, and cold drinks such as agua fresca or beer
cool the heat of chili.

## American casual meals

Casual American combos pair a burger or sandwich with fries and a soft drink; fried chicken comes
with coleslaw, biscuits, or macaroni and cheese. In the South, "meat and three" is one meat with
three vegetable or starch sides. A Hawaiian plate lunch serves a protein such as loco moco or kalua
pork with two scoops of rice and macaroni salad. Pickles, coleslaw, and cold carbonated drinks are
the usual counterweights to fried and cheesy food.

## Canadian and Quebec comfort food

Poutine (fries with cheese curds and gravy) is rich, salty, and soft-meets-crisp. It is eaten as a
meal or shared as a side, and pairs well with something acidic or crisp such as pickles, coleslaw, or
a fresh salad, or with a cold beer or soda. Smoked meat sandwiches traditionally come with a pickle
and coleslaw.

## Drinks with food

Carbonated drinks and beer cut through fried and fatty food. Milk, lassi, and other dairy drinks
soothe chili heat. Green and oolong tea refresh the palate between oily or dim sum dishes. Acidic
wines and sparkling drinks lift rich dishes, while a sweet drink next to a sweet dessert is too
much sugar at once.
